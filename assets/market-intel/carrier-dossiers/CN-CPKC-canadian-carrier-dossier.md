# CN & CPKC — Canadian Carrier Dossier

**Why these are separate from the US carriers:** not just geography. Canada regulates wayside detection prescriptively — detector spacing is a legal requirement on Key Routes, and a confirmed bearing defect triggers a codified speed restriction. That converts our product from "risk reduction" (the US pitch) into "documented compliance evidence" (the Canadian pitch). Same physics, different buyer, different argument.

## 1. The two networks

| | CN | CPKC |
|---|---|---|
| Route miles | ~20,000 (19,500 per 2026 Fact Book) | ~20,000 |
| Reach | Atlantic (Nova Scotia) to Pacific (BC), plus US Midwest and Gulf Coast | **Only single-line network linking Canada, US and Mexico** |
| Provinces | 8: BC, AB, SK, MB, ON, QC, NB, NS | BC, AB, SK, MB, ON, QC (+ US Midwest, Mexico) |
| Employees | ~24,000 | ~20,000 |
| HQ | Montreal | Calgary |
| Mexico access | Via interchange (KCSM/CPKC, UP at Laredo) | **Own track** — KCSM ~3,315 mi, Lázaro Cárdenas + Gulf ports |
| Notable | Largest rail network in Canada; connects 3 coasts | 30+ ports, 30+ automotive facilities |

**CN's structural advantage** is that it reaches three coasts on its own track and is the shortest route between the Pacific Northwest and Chicago. Its exposure is that it is heavily grain- and bulk-dependent, with a large share of traffic on lines that are single-track and seasonal.

**CPKC's structural advantage** is that it is the only carrier that can put a car on one railroad from a Mexican port to a Canadian terminal. That creates an unusual risk profile: a bearing failure originating in Mexico on CPKC-owned track, becoming a US incident, becoming a Canadian liability. **Three regulators, one failure.** No other carrier we're targeting has that.

## 2. The Canadian regulatory frame — the actual sales argument

This is the part that makes the Canadian conversation different, and better for us.

**Transport Canada, *Rules Respecting Key Trains and Key Routes* (2016):**

- A **Key Train** must not travel more than **40 miles on a Key Route without a valid inspection by a wayside defective bearing detector (WDBD)**.
- When a WDBD reports a defective bearing, the railway **must inspect that bearing**. If confirmed defective, the Key Train is **restricted to 15 mph maximum until the car is set off**.
- If a same-car defect recurs, speed is restricted to **30 mph until the next WDBD**.
- Rail flaw inspections required **at least twice annually** on main and subdivision track portions of Key Routes.
- A **risk assessment** is required under section 6, plus periodic review.

**Read that against the US regime for contrast.** In the US, HBD installation and inspection is *not* FRA-regulated — AAR Circular OT-55-R carries recommended practices, and NTSB has only *recommended* that FRA impose requirements (R-24-02 to R-24-05). Spacing of 40–64 miles is an industry norm, not a rule.

**So the Canadian buyer already has a legal obligation, and it is stricter than the American practice.** Canada mandates 40 miles. The US industry norm stretches to 64.

**Three things follow, and they're the whole pitch:**

1. **The compliance burden is real and recurring**, not a one-time capital decision. Every Key Train movement on a Key Route generates a detection-record obligation.
2. **A confirmed defect triggers a codified 15 mph restriction** — the cost of a false positive is severe and quantifiable (train-hours, crew hours, network knock-on), and so is the cost of a false negative.
3. **Our reviewer-disposition record is exactly the artifact a regulator asks for.** Not "we have a detector" — *"here is the timestamp, the alarm, the reviewer identity, the disposition, and the set-off location for every flagged bearing on this Key Route this quarter."* That is auditable evidence, and no legacy HBD network produces it in that form.

**The US pitch is "prevent a derailment." The Canadian pitch is "prove you met the rule, every time, and prove you acted on what it told you."** The second is a procurement line item that survives a budget cut because it's tied to a legal obligation. That's a stronger position than we have with BNSF.

## 3. Where the volume is

- **CN:** the Edmonton–Winnipeg–Chicago corridor and the Vancouver gateway are the dense segments. Grain on the Prairies is high-volume but seasonal and spread across a wide single-track network — **bad for corridor pricing on our site-fee model**, good for yard and terminal deployments.
- **CPKC:** the Calgary–Regina–Winnipeg–Chicago spine, plus the Mexico leg (Laredo–Monterrey–San Luis Potosí–Lázaro Cárdenas). The Mexico leg is the most interesting commercially and the hardest to sell into, because KCSM operations sit under Mexican regulatory jurisdiction (ARTF) as well as CPKC's.

**On our site-fee arithmetic:** both networks are ~20,000 route miles — roughly two-thirds of BNSF's 32,500. A full-network deployment is enormous, and neither carrier will start there. **The Canadian entry point is a Key Route segment, not a network** — and because Canada mandates detection at 40 miles, the site count on a given Key Route is roughly `route miles ÷ 40`, which is *lower* than the equivalent US corridor and easier to price honestly.

**Note the asymmetry with the US carriers:** Canada's 40-mile legal spacing means we cannot claim a corridor needs *fewer* sites than the rule requires. That constrains our pricing flexibility but removes an objection — we are not upselling detector density beyond what the regulator already mandates.

## 5. Competitive and political context

- **CPKC's Mexico exposure is a genuine differentiator and a genuine risk.** A single-line Mexico–Canada service is a strong commercial story; it also means a wheel-bearing failure can cross three jurisdictions. Insurance and hazmat routing are sensitive to exactly that.
- **CN has been publicly associated with the 2023 derailment near Boston Bar, BC** and other incidents; verify specific cases before citing them. Do not assert an incident in the room without a source in hand.
- **Canadian grain seasonality** means a deployment that proves itself in Q4 grain peak has a natural internal champion.
- Neither carrier has a merger overhang. **Unlike UP/NS, these are buyable now** — no pre-closing capex restrictions, no STB proceeding.

## 6. What we do not know

- **Whether CN or CPKC operates acoustic bearing detectors** in addition to conventional HBDs. The 40-mile rule is satisfied by WDBD; acoustic is optional. This changes the integration story substantially.
- **Their actual Key Route designations** — published at a high level, not line-by-line. Ask.
- **Whether the 15 mph restriction regime is experienced as a major operational cost.** It almost certainly is on a high-density Key Route; we should confirm rather than assume.
- **Mexican regulatory requirements for KCSM** — ARTF rules not researched here. Needed before any CPKC Mexico conversation.

## 7. Sequencing — where Canada sits

**These are the two carriers that can buy now.** No merger, no capex freeze, and a legal obligation that makes the purchase defensible.

If the BNSF and UP/NS conversations stall on merger politics through 2027, **CN and CPKC are the fallback — and arguably the better first target**, because the Canadian compliance framing is a cleaner story than the US risk-reduction framing. The counter-argument is market size: CN plus CPKC is ~40,000 route miles against a combined UP/NS of 50,000+ and BNSF's 32,500. But a signed pilot with CN is worth more than a stalled conversation with BNSF.

**The honest recommendation:** keep BNSF first because the merger litigation gives us an opening that will not exist in eighteen months, but start the Canadian track in parallel rather than treating it as a fallback. The argument writes itself, the buyer has no freeze, and the regulatory hook is the strongest we have with any carrier.
